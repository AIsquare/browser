## Overview

mRNA vaccines inject cells with instructions to generate a protein that is normally found on the surface of SARS-CoV-2, the virus that causes COVID-19. This protein can cause an immune response without a person ever having been exposed to the virus. Later, if the person is exposed to the virus, their immune system will recognize it and respond to it. mRNA vaccines are safe and cannot alter your DNA, and you cannot get COVID-19 from the vaccine. Although mRNA vaccines may seem to have arrived quickly, this technology is built on decades of scientific research that have made these vaccines a reality. 

Currently, vaccines for COVID-19, the disease caused by the SARS-CoV-2 coronavirus, are the only authorized or approved mRNA vaccines. These vaccines work by introducing a piece of mRNA that corresponds to a viral protein, specifically the spike protein found on the virus’s outer membrane. The immune system recognizes the protein as foreign and produces specialized proteins called antibodies. Once produced, antibodies remain in the body, enabling the immune system to respond quickly if exposed again. 

![A healthcare provider gives a patient a shot in their arm](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/-/scassets/images/org/health/articles/21898-mrna-vaccine-2028301.jpg)

Like all vaccines in the United States, mRNA vaccines require authorization or approval from the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) before they can be used. The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the urgent need for swift and safe vaccine development during public health emergencies. Building on the success of mRNA vaccines developed in response to the COVID-19 outbreak, the UK government partnered with Moderna in December 2022 to ensure the UK is at the forefront of this potentially transformational technology.

## Context

Researchers have been developing mRNA technology for decades, allowing scientists to create safe, effective vaccines faster than ever before. This means that when there’s an outbreak of severe illness, we can potentially have the protection of vaccines in a matter of months instead of years. mRNA vaccines are also more adaptable than other types of vaccines, meaning they can be updated or revised quickly to protect against new viral variants. However, safety and efficacy testing of new vaccines follows a regulated path that is no different for mRNA vaccines than for any other new vaccine.

Research into mRNA technology dates back to the 1970s, with scientists first applying it to vaccine development in the 1990s. The method used in the first FDA-approved mRNA drug, patisiran (Onpattro®), paved the way for Moderna and Pfizer-BioNTech to use it in their COVID vaccines. It took over 20 years of research to learn how to get our immune systems to recognize the mRNA without destroying it too quickly and how to get it into our cells.

## Mechanism

mRNA vaccines use synthetic mRNA—safely engineered in a laboratory to mimic natural biological processes—to teach cells how to make a protein, or a small part of a protein, from a specific pathogen that triggers an immune response. The mRNA in these vaccines carries the instructions for making a harmless piece of protein identical to one found in a particular virus or bacterium, allowing the immune system to recognize it. 

Once the mRNA is delivered into the body through a lipid nanoparticle, it enters the host cell and is translated into the coded antigen. The antigen can then stimulate the immune system in various ways, including being presented on the cell surface to stimulate T cells and B cells. After the protein is made, the mRNA is quickly broken down by the body, ensuring it does not alter DNA or remain in the body for long.

## Structure

mRNA vaccines are composed of synthetic nucleic acid bases (adenine, uracil, guanine, and cytosine) that encode a sequence for a specific protein. The mRNA is transcribed from DNA in vitro and capped with a poly-adenine tail to prevent degradation. The output is purified and put into a delivery mechanism, typically lipid nanoparticles, which facilitate the entry of mRNA into cells.

## Examples

mRNA vaccines have low toxicity because they are quickly degraded by normal cellular processes. They are a non-infectious alternative to other vaccine types, meaning there is no possibility for an infection to occur from the vaccine itself. However, mRNA vaccines need to be kept at cold temperatures for storage and transport, which presents challenges, especially in low and middle-income countries. 

Safety concerns have emerged regarding mild-to-moderate myocarditis and pericarditis following mRNA vaccination, mainly in young male adults, albeit without fatal consequences. Close monitoring of this safety signal will be crucial in the development of future mRNA vaccines. 

mRNA vaccines can be used prophylactically to prevent infection or disease, or therapeutically to support the immune response after infection. They are particularly well-suited for targeting viruses, as they are structurally smaller and less complex than bacteria or parasites. 

## Limits

The FDA approval process for mRNA vaccines involves careful review of clinical trial data to confirm that a vaccine is safe and effective. Before they were rolled out, the mRNA vaccines approved for use against COVID-19 were extensively tested in large-scale clinical trials. Both vaccines have reported minor side effects, such as flu-like symptoms, that resolve within one or two days. 

There is no risk of an mRNA vaccine changing your DNA because mRNA does not have the ability to alter DNA. Serious side effects are extremely rare and far outweighed by the risks of the diseases they are designed to protect against. 

Side effects of mRNA COVID vaccines include pain or swelling at the injection site, fatigue, headache, muscle aches, and fever. In rare instances, allergic reactions can occur, including skin rash, itching, hives, and swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or throat (anaphylaxis).

## Related

Traditional vaccines work by giving a person either viral proteins or an inactivated or weakened version of a virus that triggers an immune response. In contrast, mRNA vaccines provide the instructions for our bodies to produce parts of the virus within our own cells, which triggers our immune system to mount a response if it encounters the pathogen again.
